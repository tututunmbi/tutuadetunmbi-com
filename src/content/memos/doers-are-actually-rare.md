I’ve been thinking about how rare it is to meet people who actually do the things they say they want to do, and the more people I meet, the more I realize that having an idea and being willing to get up and do something about it are very different things.

These rare species of executors don’t have everything figured out, but they are willing to f\*\*k around and find out by doing. The *“let’s just see how it goes”* people. The ones whose *“f\*\*k it, we ball”* is followed by an email, a proposal, content created, a payment, or whatever the thing in front of them requires.

I recognize those people because, for better or worse, I am a *“get up and do”* person I woke up one day and decided I wanted to do an event at Landmark, and I went ahead and did it. Yes, it almost killed me, and there are probably several things I would approach differently now, but the event happened. I’ve sent proposals with amounts that required me to sit with myself for a moment before pressing send. I’ve reached out to people about partnerships because I could see the possibility of something, even when I had no idea whether they would see it too. And sometimes, I did some audacious things purely for the love of the game.

*“Doing it for the plot”* sounds fun until the plot gets messy, has you crying, resenting, cringing and confused for a month. Sometimes the plot can outdo you, other times, you can find yourself in some wonderful opportunities, meet new characters and, have a plot twist that completely reframes your life experience.

Sometimes it’s much smaller than that. **I’ve always felt responsible for what I get out of the world and what the world gets out of me, and boy, am I impatient and hungry for what I believe I deserve.** That impatience shows up in ordinary ways: sending the message when somebody crosses my mind, asking about an opportunity instead of assuming I won’t get it, getting on Canva or ChatGPT when I have something to say and my designer is busy. **My inability to ask for help has sometimes made things harder than they needed to be, but I’ve always been willing to get involved in making something happen. And there are things I do purely for the love of the game, because I’m curious, because I want to see whether an idea can become something outside my own head.**

So perhaps that’s why I notice how much time we can spend talking around the things we want. We **plan** them, **explain** them, **ask** people what they think, **imagine** how successful they could become, and sometimes even start preparing for all the ways they might fail before we’ve made a single move.

The funny thing is that I’m a strategic consultant, so thinking things through is part of what I do for a living. I understand the value of a plan. But I also speak to enough people to recognize when **we’re using another conversation about the idea to postpone the moment we’ll actually have to attempt it.** Sometimes we’re on the fifth conversation, and the email we agreed needed to be sent in the first one is still sitting in drafts ⍨.

I met a young guy who reminded me of this recently. He had reached out to me about an issue with his university, and I tried to help him resolve it, but I couldn’t. I felt bad about that, so when I saw him at a mixer some time later and he came over to hug me, I asked how things had turned out.

He said, *“Oh, that one,” almost* nonchalantly and then started telling me everything he had been doing since we last spoke. He had worked with a top bank in Nigeria and was about to begin an internship somewhere else, in one of the Teir-1 law firms and as he talked, I became more interested in how he had made all of this happen.

So I asked him, and his explanation was so ordinary that I think some people would have been disappointed by it. He DOES. He cold emails people. He sends DMs. He sees someone doing something he finds interesting and reaches out. Sometimes he starts by giving them a genuine compliment because everything he does comes from genuine love of the game. He puts himself in a position where a conversation can happen, and some of those conversations have led to opportunities. In a nutshell, he just lived by doing. By putting more of himself out there to the world, and expecting that all these will find alignment in his journey. And it did!

> I recognized that spirit immediately, because I know what it’s like to have someone ask you how you made something happen and feel almost unhelpful when you explain it. You’re trying to give them an answer they can use, but the answer is simply that you reached out, or asked, or applied, or decided to try with what you had available. There were things you couldn’t have known beforehand, and you learned them because you had already started.

**I think about it in the same way I think about being hungry. At some point, I know I have to get up and look for food. I can sit there discussing what I’d like to eat for as long as I want, but eventually I’m going to have to move…**

That hunger has taken me into rooms I wasn’t entirely sure I belonged in yet. It has made me send messages without knowing whether I would get a response, invest in access when money was tight, and put an idea in front of someone who had every right to say no. Some of those decisions worked out beautifully, and some gave me information I would have preferred to acquire under less expensive circumstances. But they gave me something to work with.

And I think there’s something quite painful about knowing you have the ability to do something and repeatedly watching yourself postpone it. You end up in this perpetual purgatory of unmet potential, where you can describe the person you could become in extraordinary detail, but your days contain very little evidence that you’re giving her a chance.

Then the comparison starts, because while you’ve been thinking about it, somebody else has been attempting it. You’re watching them learn, improve, find their people, and get opportunities, and even though you might be happy for them, their progress starts reminding you of something you’ve been avoiding in yourself. Sometimes you know you could do what they’re doing. You might even believe you could do it better, which is a particularly uncomfortable thought to sit with when they’ve actually put something into the world and you’re still explaining what yours is going to look like.

And then comes the grief for the timeline you thought you would be living by now. Every time you consider starting, **you’re judging your beginning against where you imagine you would have been if you had started three years ago.** You’re already disappointed in the first attempt because, in your head, you should be much further along. So you postpone it again, and another year passes, and now there’s even more distance between where you are and where you believe you should have been.

I can imagine how exhausting it must become to carry that version of yourself around, the one who has done all the things you keep saying you’re going to do. You’ve spoken about her so often that people ask how the business is coming along, or when the project is launching, and even an innocent question begins to feel like something you have to recover from. Eventually, you start avoiding the subject, because you’re tired of hearing yourself give another explanation.

> And the strange thing is that when you go looking for inspiration to get yourself out of that feeling, look at who you turn to. *****The doers.***** You’re downloading their guides, saving their videos, buying their workbooks, their planners, their courses. You’re studying the things they made because they got up and did something, hoping that something in their work will finally get you to do yours. By the time you’ve downloaded the guide, bought the planner, and finished the course, the only thing you still haven’t done is the thing you bought all of them to help you do.

I understand how that can happen, because preparation gives you somewhere comfortable to put your hope. There’s always something else to learn, and **learning can feel enough like progress that you don’t immediately notice how long you’ve been standing in the same place.** But at some point, you have to give yourself an experience of your own. Send the proposal and find out what they say. Put the offer out and see whether anybody wants it. Give yourself something real to respond to, because **it’s difficult to keep judging your entire life against a version of yourself who has never had to attempt anything outside your imagination.**

**…**

And even among the people who start, there’s another kind of doing that becomes necessary when things don’t go the way you imagined. When you’ve put the offer out and there are no sales, or the partnership doesn’t happen, or you discover that people don’t want the thing in quite the way you thought they would, you have to decide what to do with that information. You might need to change the offer, ask better questions, try another approach, or accept that this particular idea has gone as far as it can go.

That part asks a lot of you, because by then you’re dealing with your own disappointment as well as the actual work. It’s easy to say *“f\*\*k it, we ball”* when you’re excited and everybody is cheering you on. You begin to understand what it means when you’ve had a terrible week and still have to sit down and work out your next move.

I’m learning to pay closer attention to the people who have that spirit, including the ones doing things quietly, on a scale that might not impress anybody yet. When I meet someone who follows through, who comes back having tried something, who can tell me what happened and what they’re going to do about it, I want to know more. There is so much you can build with a person like that.

And whenever I find myself spending too long explaining something I want to do, I have to ask myself the same question: *what have I actually done about it?* Sometimes the answer is embarrassing, especially for someone who has this much to say about doing. But I also think about how much of what has helped me came from somebody who went ahead with an idea they could just as easily have spent the rest of their life talking about. Somewhere, somebody is using their work to find the courage to begin, and they might never even know it. I wonder who could have found that courage through something I’m still making excuses for.

Doers are actually rare, and somebody who needs what you keep postponing is still waiting for you to become one.
