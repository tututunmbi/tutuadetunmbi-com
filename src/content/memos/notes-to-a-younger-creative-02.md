The next time you catch yourself complaining about a client’s behaviour or stressful antics in the group chat, or to a friend, I want you to pay attention to how well you’re able to explain the problem to a complete stranger. You know when it started. You know what the client did, what they should have done instead, and what’s likely to happen in another month if they carry on like this. You’ve probably explained it so well that somebody who has never met this person now has a working understanding of their business.

#### *But have you said any of it to the client?…?*

I ask because I know how easy it is to have the full conversation everywhere else. Sometimes, quite early in a job, you start noticing things that nobody asked you to look at. You notice what gets the client’s attention and what they keep putting off. You sit in a meeting and realize that the people who are supposed to carry the work forward haven’t been told why it’s happening. You find out that a decision everyone is waiting on can only be made by one person, and that person takes two weeks to answer an email. And sometimes you can see, with surprising clarity, how a project or business will fall apart *after* you’ve done your work beautifully and handed it over.

So you do your part. You deliver the strategy, the identity, the website, whatever they brought you in to do. Everybody is pleased on the day you hand it over, and you’re pleased too, because the work is good. But you’ve already wondered who’s going to use it, whether they’ll have what they need to use it well, and what will happen the first time the client gets busy and goes back to doing things the old way. Six months later, somebody asks how the project went, and you can explain exactly what happened. **The uncomfortable part is how much of that explanation you could have given on the day they hired you.**

And because you can still do your part well, it’s easy to tell yourself that doing it well is enough. You hand over the work, everybody is pleased, and maybe for a while you are too. Then you check in a few months later and find that the staff are still doing things the old way, or that the person who was supposed to get everything moving is still waiting for an answer. And what gets you is how familiar it all sounds. You’ve already had this conversation. You just had it with your friends.

> I think the best creatives I know are willing to have that conversation much earlier. They’ll sit with a client, listen to what they want, and say, *“Okay, and here’s what I’m going to need from you for any of that to happen.”* They say it while the client still has time to do something with the information.

**This sounds straightforward until you’re the one who has to tell someone who is about to pay you that they may be part of the reason the work won’t do what they want it to do.** I understand why it’s easier to say, “*I’m just here to do the website.”* And sometimes the website really is your whole and only business. But if they’re hiring you to build one that brings in enquiries, and you can already see that nobody answers the enquiries they get now, I think that’s something to bring up before you spend three months building them a nicer place to ignore people from.

And the funny thing is, the website might do exactly what you built it to do. It might bring in twice as many enquiries, and now there are twice as many people waiting for an answer. I think that can happen with almost anything we make as a creative. You can give a business a beautiful new way to explain what it does, and the staff can still be hearing about it for the first time from a customer. You can bring more people through the door, and more people will get to experience the very thing you knew would make them leave.

**Sometimes your work succeeds just enough to make the problem you kept quiet about harder to miss.** And when the client finally asks you why everybody’s complaining, you have to decide how surprised you’re going to pretend to be.

And this is where I get a little disappointed with *****“I’m just here to do my work,”***** because I think some of us have made *our work* very small so we can feel quite good about having done it. If you’re there for the money, fair enough. But if you’re wondering why people pay you, tell you how much they love what you made, and never call you again, or why you keep handing over work you’re proud of and still feel strangely unsatisfied, I think you already know part of the answer. You knew your work needed **their** work too. **You knew what they would have to do with what you made for it to matter, and you left without having that conversation.** I think that’s where mediocrity can hide, even in beautiful work.

### That’s what I mean by the sinfulness of a stoic creative.

> **I’m talking about the particular composure you can mistake for professionalism when you’ve seen something that matters and decided to keep it to yourself.** Somebody trusted you enough to bring you close to their business. Part of your gift is being able to see what it could become, and sometimes that same gift makes it painfully obvious what’s getting in the way. I think you owe the work enough devotion to say so.

And this is where I have to ask what **you** came for, because the willingness to say what you need starts with knowing what would make the work worthwhile to you.

For me, I love seeing what happens when somebody takes what I am being paid to do for them seriously. I love when people start approaching the business differently, when the enquiries change, when other people begin to see something in the brand that I could see back when we were still talking about it in private. That’s part of what I want from the work. So if I know it requires the client to take their offline strategy seriously, or to show up consistently after the excitement of the launch wears off, I have to say that. I can hardly expect them to share my idea of a good outcome if we’ve never spoken about what it will ask of them.

I’m a brand strategist and I run a strategic consultancy, and one of the hardest parts of my work is that I can sometimes see a brand becoming something bigger than even the founder had imagined. I love that part, but it means that before we agree to work together, I have to say, *“Look, if we make this look like a five-star experience online, people are going to come in expecting five stars. What happens when they actually get here?”* Because **if we’re going to build an otherworldly brand, we’re going to need an otherworldly experience to go with it.** Your entire company has to know what we promised, and somebody has to give them what they need to deliver it.

And if I can already see that you’re more interested in looking like that brand online than in doing the work it would take to be that brand in person, I’m going to find it very hard to take the job, and I owe you an explanation. People might come because the photographs were beautiful, but they’ll write the review about what happened when they got there. Some won’t write anything at all. They’ll just never come back. And if I can see that possibility while we’re still sitting here talking about what you want, **how am I helping you get it by keeping that to myself?**

*Because guess who that same client is going to pour all their projections on when shit hits the fan?…Me!*

**So…what do YOU want? My darling young creative…or are you just taking anything that lands on your lap with no end game?**

Maybe what you want from this particular job is to be paid well, and that’s a good enough reason to take it. But suppose you’re taking it because you want more work in that industry. When the job is done and you’re sitting across from the next client, what will you be able to show them? Can you put the project in your portfolio? Will you get to see what happened after the work went live, so you can talk about what it actually did for the business? Those are things you can ask for while you’re still deciding whether to take the job, because you know what it would take to see the project actually live up to the expectations that YOU want to show the world that you are capable of.

*The end game can be selfish, and you can still be selfless.* I can want to serve in a palace because I know it will put me in front of kings. And once I’m there, I can care enough about what I’ve been brought in to do to say, *“My king, there are a few things happening here that are going to make it very difficult for us to get where you say you want to go.”* I’d choose my moment, obviously. I am trying to work in another palace after this one. But I’d also like to be able to say I did something useful while I was there.

What I want you to understand is that once you know what you want from a job, it gets easier to say what you need before you take it. And I need you to know what you want out of the work you are doing, so it is easier to say what you need before you take it!

> I know creatives who pay close attention to how long it takes someone to reply during the first conversation, because they know what that silence will mean when there’s a whole team waiting on one answer. Some want a business that has been around long enough to know its customers, because they don’t want a client that believes they should reach *“everyone online”*. Some need one person who can make a decision, and some would be deeply concerned to discover that the same person makes *every* decision. **They know what happens to their work under certain conditions, and they’ve learnt to ask about those conditions while everybody still has the freedom to say,** *****“This might be a terrible arrangement for us.”*****

So before you say yes to the next job, ask yourself what you want from it and what the client will have to do for the work to stand a chance. Then tell them.

*“If the goal is sales, who’s following up when people ask how to buy?”* is a perfectly reasonable question to ask before you agree to spend the next six months making people want to buy. **And if you’re already halfway into a job and you’ve been rehearsing the same complaint for three weeks, take the version you’ve managed to explain so clearly to your friends and find a way to say it to the person it concerns.**

They may disagree with you. They may agree and still do very little about it. You can give the work everything it reasonably asks of you and still reach a point where you have to recognize that the other person’s share is theirs. If that means finishing what you agreed to do and letting them go, at least both of you will understand why.

What I’d hate is for you to get very good at seeing what could change in a room and spend your whole career explaining it in the room you went to afterwards. By then, the client has paid you, thanked you, and gone back to doing things the way they’ve always done them. And the clearest explanation of why the work never had a chance is still in the group chat…
